**The carbon price politicians dare not mention**

It has become fashionable to dismiss carbon pricing as a relic of technocratic optimism. Yet the facts stubbornly refuse to cooperate. Britain's carbon price floor, introduced in 2013, helped push coal off the grid: emissions from power generation have since fallen by roughly three-quarters. British Columbia's carbon tax, levied since 2008, was followed by faster economic growth than in the rest of Canada, even as emissions per head fell.

The problem is not that green technology is too expensive; it is that fossil fuels remain too cheap. Solar panels and batteries are now the cheapest source of new electricity in most countries. What is missing is not invention but incentive: as long as polluting is free, cleaner alternatives will struggle to displace it.

Critics warn that carbon taxes would cripple growth and punish the poor. The evidence suggests otherwise — and where the burden does bite, the answer is to recycle the revenue as dividends, not to abandon the instrument altogether. Even if every country honoured its current pledges in full, the world would still warm by around 2.1°C; pricing carbon is therefore not radical but the moderate minimum.

Politicians who dodge the question are not sparing voters pain, merely postponing it. A price on carbon is the rare climate policy that is both efficient and honest. The sooner it stops being unsayable, the better.
